# Private AI: seeing where your model calls actually go

*Trace Commons 0.11.0 — 2026-09-07*

There's a particular frustration in switching something on and having no
idea whether it worked. The toggle says it's on. Nothing you can see is
any different. You go back to work and quietly assume it's fine.

That was the state of answering model calls on your own machine until
this release. You could turn it on and there was no honest way to tell
whether a single call had ever come through.

0.11 gives it a place of its own: **Private AI**, a top-level screen on
macOS, Windows and Linux. Your AI calls go out through this computer, so
you can see them and choose who answers.

Think of it as a VPN for AI, and hold that analogy honestly. Your calls
travel out through a point you control. That point sees them and keeps
the record. It doesn't hide them from whoever answers — the call still
goes on to the provider you chose. What's changed is that the record is
yours, and so is the choice.

## Where the name comes from

One of the destinations registered by default is NEAR AI's
[Private Inference](https://docs.near.ai/cloud/private-inference), which
runs models inside hardware trusted execution environments: Intel TDX for
the virtual machine, NVIDIA's GPU TEE for the model and its computation,
and the TLS connection terminated inside the enclave rather than at a load
balancer. In their words, it "ensures that AI computations happen in a
completely isolated environment where no one—not the cloud provider, not
the model provider, not even NEAR AI can access your data," verifiable
"through cryptographic attestation." That's their claim about their
hardware, and their page explains how to check it yourself.

Note where that property lives. It belongs to the destination, not to
this screen. A call answered by NEAR AI gets it; a call your tool sends
straight to Anthropic or OpenAI gets whatever those services offer. The
screen adds no confidentiality to anything. It's where you see your calls
and pick somewhere that has some.

And one distinction worth keeping straight, because the two ideas sit
close together: a session you contribute to Trace Commons is shared with
Trace Commons. That's the whole product, and it's your call, session by
session. TEE confidentiality is about who can read a call while it's
being answered. It says nothing about what you later decide to share.

## What you'll find there

The screen lists the coding tools on your computer — Claude Code and
Codex today — and tells you, for each one, whether it's connected,
whether anything has arrived from it, or whether it's genuinely
answering.

That last distinction is the point of the whole release. A settings file
with the right value in it proves only that a file has a value in it. It
is not evidence that one call was ever answered. Now you can tell the
difference at a glance.

Connecting a tool happens one decision at a time, behind a preview. The
app shows you the exact change it would make to that tool's own settings
file and writes nothing until you say so. If you'd already put something
in that setting yourself, it says so and leaves it alone. It won't
overwrite your choices.

The switch is also in the tray — the menu bar on macOS — and on a
keyboard shortcut, with one deliberate asymmetry. Turning it *off* acts
immediately, because that only ever reduces what your computer will
answer. Turning it *on* opens the screen first. While it's on, anything
else running on your computer can send calls through it, charged to the
accounts you've set up. That's worth deciding with the consequence in
front of you rather than from a menu.

## The limits, plainly

**Two tools, not four.** Gemini isn't supported: the mechanism can write
one of two API shapes and neither is Gemini's. Cline keeps no settings
file we can find. We'd rather say so than imply more is on the way.

**It does nothing until you connect something.** Turning the switch on,
by itself, changes where no tool sends its calls.

**Despite the name, the screen promises no privacy.** Whatever
confidentiality your calls have comes from where they're answered, not
from this feature — pick a destination without it and there is none. The
screen moves where your calls are answered, keeps the record on your
computer, and shows you the choice. The destination still sees what you
send it.

## Along the way

Building this meant depending on IronWire, the local component that does
the answering, and depending on something closely is how you find out
what's wrong with it. We found and fixed seven defects there, all merged
upstream ([#33](https://github.com/nearai/ironwire/issues/33),
[#34](https://github.com/nearai/ironwire/issues/34),
[#37](https://github.com/nearai/ironwire/issues/37),
[#39](https://github.com/nearai/ironwire/issues/39),
[#40](https://github.com/nearai/ironwire/issues/40),
[#42](https://github.com/nearai/ironwire/issues/42),
[#45](https://github.com/nearai/ironwire/issues/45)).

Two are worth naming. Connecting a tool could move a setting you'd
already chosen. And a preview showing you additions could commit a
removal. Those fixes reach you whether or not you ever open this screen.

## Getting it

The desktop app updates itself. The CLI is on Homebrew.
